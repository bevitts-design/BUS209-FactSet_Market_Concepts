BUS209 · Fixed income
03

The root of the bond market

A bond turns a borrowing promise into a tradable security

Principal

The amount borrowed and repaid at maturity.

Coupon

The contractual interest paid to the lender.

Maturity

The date when principal is due.

Yield

The return investors use to compare bonds.

Why the market matters

Governments and companies rely on bonds to finance spending and investment. U.S. Treasury securities also serve as a liquid benchmark for pricing risk across global markets.

BUS209 · Bond valuation
04

The required return on lending

Bond yields compensate investors for time, inflation, and credit risk

TimeMaturity

Longer lending periods expose investors to more uncertainty and changing rates.

Purchasing powerInflation

Higher expected inflation reduces the real value of fixed coupon and principal payments.

RepaymentCredit

Weaker borrower creditworthiness requires a higher yield to compensate for default risk.

FactSet route: use yield, spread, ratings, and debt-capital-structure evidence together—not as isolated numbers.
BUS209 · Monetary policy
05

Policy enters the pricing process

Central banks shape short-term rates and market expectations

The policy objective

Central banks use interest rates and communication to contain inflation, support economic stability, and influence financial conditions.

Bond investors watch both the decision and what it signals about the future.

Higher policy rates

Raise short-term borrowing costs and can slow demand.

Inflation expectations

Influence the longer end of the yield curve.

Forward guidance

Can move yields before the policy rate actually changes.

BUS209 · Yield curve
06

Borrowing costs across maturities

The yield curve combines current policy with expectations about the future

OvernightPolicy anchor

The central bank has the strongest direct influence.

Short termNext moves

Markets price the likely path of policy rates.

IntermediateCycle outlook

Growth and inflation expectations become more important.

Long termRisk premium

Inflation uncertainty and duration risk shape yields.

Why it matters: government yields become benchmarks for mortgages, corporate bonds, and global financing costs.
BUS209 · Live practice
07

You interpret the signal

Curve shape reveals what the market expects—not what must happen

SteepLong yields > short

Often consistent with stronger growth, rising inflation expectations, or both.

FlatSimilar yields

Can signal uncertainty about the next phase of the economic cycle.

InvertedShort yields > long

Often reflects restrictive policy and expectations of slower future growth.

Discuss: Which additional evidence would you check before treating an inverted curve as a recession signal?

BUS209 · Currency valuation
12

Relative conditions drive relative value

Inflation, growth, and interest-rate differentials shape currency demand

InflationPurchasing power

Higher expected inflation can weaken demand for a currency when its future purchasing power falls.

GrowthCapital flows

Stronger expected growth can attract investment into a country’s equity and bond markets.

Rate differentialRelative yield

Higher yields may attract currency inflows when inflation and credit risk remain credible.

Analytical rule: a higher rate is not automatically bullish if it reflects unstable inflation or rising country risk.
BUS209 · FX quotations
13

Read the base and terms currencies carefully

A currency quote tells you how many units of one currency buy another

USD/JPY = 138.80

One U.S. dollar buys 138.80 Japanese yen.

USD/MXN = 17.5515

One U.S. dollar buys 17.5515 Mexican pesos.

Common exception

EUR, GBP, AUD, and NZD are often quoted with the non-USD currency as the base.

Build a cross rate

JPY per MXN = 138.80 ÷ 17.5515
≈ 7.9082 JPY per MXN

The shared USD base cancels when the two quotes are divided.

BUS209 · Central bank intervention
14

Three policy channels

Central banks can influence currencies through markets, communication, or rates

Direct interventionBuy or sell currency

Use foreign reserves to increase or decrease demand in the FX market.

Verbal interventionSignal intent

Try to move expectations without immediately spending reserves; credibility is essential.

Rate interventionChange relative yield

Alter policy rates to influence capital flows and demand for the currency.

BUS209 · Policy trade-offs
15

The objective depends on the economy

A stronger currency helps importers; a weaker currency can help exporters

Weaker currency

Domestic goods and services become cheaper for foreign buyers, which can support export competitiveness—but imports become more expensive.

Stronger currency

Foreign goods and services cost less in domestic-currency terms, which can help importers and consumers—but exporters face tougher pricing.

Policy tension: central banks must balance inflation control, growth, financial stability, and external competitiveness.
